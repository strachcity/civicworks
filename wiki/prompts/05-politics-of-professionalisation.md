# The politics of professionalisation

**Status:** claim in hand, evidence thin, and named as unfinished business by the piece that created it. 14 makes an argument against professional boundaries and explicitly parks what it costs.

## The claim

Digital specialists in government spent two decades winning professional standing, and the argument that professional boundaries now constrain transformation asks them to loosen the thing that protects them. Anyone making that argument has to say who carries the cost, or it gets heard as a case for generalists running everything again, which is the arrangement the professions were built to escape.

## Why it can be written now

**14 ends on it and does not touch it.** "What this means for the politics of professionalisation, when digital specialists spent years fighting for the standing that professions gave them." That is the question the essay's own proposition raises and defers.

**The history is already in the corpus, in one footnote.** Dunleavy, Margetts, Bastow and Tinkler on technical specialists kept in professional enclaves away from the top of the civil service, with UK IT occupations only recognised as a civil service occupational group on a par with others in 2004-5. Seven years before GDS. The frameworks solved a real and old exclusion.

**Both halves of the collision are published and neither meets the other.** 10 argues the profession's structures are the constraint. 14 argues professions should be platforms rather than silos. Neither says what happens to grading, pay, progression or standing when the boundary becomes permeable. M4 says the intervention is interrogating the framework; this is the framework whose interrogation has a price attached.

## Evidence in hand

Dunleavy et al on the enclaves and the 2004-5 recognition. 10's reading of the DDaT framework: "Deliver. Not shape. Not challenge. Not co-produce." 11's history of the professions and methods government adopted around iterative learning. 14's platforms-not-silos proposition and its concession that legibility gave digital capability its permanence in Whitehall.

## What is missing

**Almost all of it.** This is a position, not yet an argument.

**Whether any profession has deliberately loosened its own boundaries** and what happened to its members. Medicine, law, architecture, engineering all have versions of this fight. None is in the corpus.

**What the actual mechanisms are.** Standing in the civil service runs through grading, job families, capability frameworks and recruitment. "Permeable boundaries" has to survive contact with all four or it is a value statement.

**Anyone's account from the people who fought for the frameworks.** Whether they would trade them, and for what. Without at least one, this is a piece telling a profession what it should give up, written by someone whose own standing does not depend on the answer.

## What would make it fail

**Writing it as an argument against professions.** The claim is that the depth is the point and the boundary is the question. 14 already got this right and the prompt inherits the risk.

**Pretending the cost falls evenly.** It falls hardest on whoever is least legible without a framework to point at, which is more likely to be research and design than engineering. If the piece cannot say who loses, it has not been written.

**Making it about job titles.** Grading and recognition are the mechanism, not the subject. The subject is whether a settlement that took 20 years to win can be reopened without losing it.
