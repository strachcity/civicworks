# Who carries the cost, who captures the return

**Status:** ready, and partly a rewrite. Piece 08 made this argument in about 900 words and converted no one. A second sighting arrived Aug 2026 from an unrelated domain, which widens the claim.

## The claim

Public investment routinely absorbs early uncertainty while private actors capture scale-driven returns, and the cause is not the size of the investment but the design of the partnership. Conditionality is a design object. It is almost never designed.

**The wider version, after the public data material.** The design object is the terms on which a publicly created asset enters private value creation. Money invested is one case. A dataset created, curated and assured at public expense is another. The question is the same in both: when a public asset enables private value, what should flow back to the system that created and maintains it?

## Why it can be written now

08 has the argument and no room. Its own line: "The architecture of partnership, rather than the volume of investment, becomes the central design flaw." Then it stops.

It is also the clearest instance in the corpus of a mechanism outside M1, which matters: five of six mechanisms are about decision architecture, and a publication needs more than one spine.

**And it now has a second domain.** 08 argued this about industrial policy and public investment. Public sector data arrives at the same design question from somewhere entirely unrelated. That is the first evidence that this is a mechanism rather than a fintech observation.

## Evidence in hand

The NICE and NHS England antimicrobial subscription model, where revenue is delinked from volume and "the whole mechanism was redesigned, not just the funding level". Korea's MyData and open banking rails, 39 million users, as market-shaping in practice. David Eaves on which layers of the stack a country chooses to commodify and govern. Market failure theory as the inherited logic, which connects it to M4.

Portfolio logic (M6) is the other half and 08 predates it: the UN80 piece argues that funding should follow evidence with quarterly reallocation. Conditionality decides who captures the return, portfolio logic decides how the bets are held. They have never been argued together.

### Public data, the second sighting

Miranda Sharp, [Charging for public sector data](https://medium.com/@miranda_41537/charging-for-public-sector-data-417b626c58c8). Explores whether charging for access could improve data quality by generating revenue, sharper demand signals and a closer relationship between users and the body maintaining the asset.

**The article is the provocation, not the proof.** It argues about access charging. The mechanism only appears once you ask what the charge is standing in for, which is a feedback loop between the value of the asset and investment in maintaining it.

Three ways money can move, worth keeping apart:

- **Access pricing.** You want the asset, so you pay to reach it.
- **Cost recovery.** Your use creates curation, assurance, infrastructure or compute costs, so you cover them.
- **Value sharing.** A publicly maintained asset enables private commercial value, so some of that value returns to the public system.

The third is what connects to 08, and it is the only one that lets the asset stay broadly open for public, research, civic and non-commercial use while conditions attach where significant commercial value is extracted. Those conditions need not be financial: contributions to maintaining the underlying infrastructure, returning improvements or derived data, preferential public-sector access to the resulting product, milestone payments, royalties, revenue share.

Working formulation, not a line for the piece. Data quality may need a feedback loop. Charging for access is one possible proxy for that loop. Value sharing would try to build the loop around the value actually created.

### The NHS precedents, and what each actually demonstrates

Checked before use. Three different things, easily blurred.

- **[Value Sharing Framework for NHS data partnerships](https://transform.england.nhs.uk/key-tools-and-info/centre-improving-data-collaboration/value-sharing-framework-for-nhs-data-partnerships/)** (NHS England, Centre for Improving Data Collaboration, developed 2020 to 2023). The closest real precedent, and stronger than either source originally reached for. Guiding principles for negotiating fair terms, including that the NHS should seek a share of commercial value proportional to its contribution, agreed up front through payments or revenue royalties, and that society should be compensated for the knowledge and public assets put in. Principles for negotiators rather than a binding requirement, which is the interesting weakness.
- **DHSC data access policy.** Five principles for NHS data partnerships, including fair terms for the NHS and no exclusive arrangements. The data access policy update ties Secure Data Environments to the Framework's principles. Policy by reference rather than by mechanism.
- **[NHS IP guidance for England](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/intellectual-property-ip-guidance-for-the-nhs-in-england)** (Nov 2025), with case studies and model agreements. Weaker for this argument than it first looks. Much of the royalty material is about rewarding staff inventors, which is benefit sharing inside the organisation rather than public capture of privately extracted value. Read the case studies before citing.
- **The Code of Conduct for data-driven health and care technology** contains the right sentiment, that the NHS should obtain a fair share of the value of innovations built on its data, but it was superseded in Jan 2021 by *A Guide to Good Practice for Digital and Data-Driven Health Technologies*. Do not cite the initial code as live policy.
- **The antimicrobial model is not a value-sharing case.** It shows the terms of a market can be deliberately redesigned so public objectives and commercial incentives connect. That is market shaping, and it is evidence that terms are designable, not evidence that value returns.

## What is missing

**A UK case where conditionality was designed and failed, or was absent and cost something measurable.** Still the biggest hole, and now more obvious. There are two success-shaped examples and a framework, and nothing that went wrong.

**Whether any of this has ever returned anything.** The Value Sharing Framework documents principles. It does not document outcomes. Without one agreement that actually returned value to the public system, this is policy intent, which is the same shape as the problem the prompt is about.

**The legal and institutional mechanism for downstream reciprocity.** Attaching conditions at the point of value creation rather than the point of access is the whole proposition, and there is nothing here showing it can be done. Licence terms, statute, contract at the point of commercialisation, something else. Unknown.

**Who decides what counts as significant commercial value.** Moving the test downstream does not remove the classification problem that makes access charging awkward. It relocates it.

## What would make it fail

**Writing it as a public data piece.** This is the live danger now, and it is the same failure the fintech framing produced in 08. Public data is the occasion. The subject is what the state gets back for carrying risk and maintaining assets.

**Collapsing into "commercial users should pay for public data".** That may be exactly the wrong intervention. Charging at the point of access requires classifying users, policing licences and defining commercial use, which loads cost onto small firms and socially useful reuse while barely troubling large incumbents. The more interesting possibility is that access stays open and reciprocity attaches downstream, where substantial private value is created. That is still a proposition, and the piece should keep the uncertainty rather than resolving it.

**Reheating 08.** The Korea event framing was what capped it at 900 words in the first place.
