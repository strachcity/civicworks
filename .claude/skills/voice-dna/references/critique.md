# The critique pass

For "does this work", "tighten this", "read this back to me", and for your own drafts before you show them.

Work in this order. It is deliberately top-down: line edits to a paragraph that should not exist are wasted, and worse, they make a weak piece feel finished.

## 1. Is there an argument

- What does the piece claim that a reader could disagree with? Say it in one sentence. If you cannot, that is the finding, and it outranks everything below.
- Does it get underneath the observation? Name the mechanism it identifies: the structure, incentive, classification, relationship, funding logic, decision architecture or operating condition producing the visible problem.
- Are the common explanations given their due before the piece goes past them? They are usually partly true, and skipping them reads as a straw man.
- Is the concrete material real? Named organisations, actual decisions, things somebody did. Invented examples are a finding.
- Does the theory arrive through the subject, or is a framework announced and then filled?

## 2. Does it build

- Read only the first sentence of each paragraph, in sequence. Does an argument develop, or is it a set of related observations in an order that could be shuffled?
- Does every paragraph break mark a move, a deepening or a turn?
- Are the transitions causal, an unspoken "but", "so", "therefore"? Or are there hinge sentences announcing a turn instead of making one?
- Does the introduction do the diagnostic work and then the first section do it again? Each section gets one job.
- Does a strong formulation land more than once? It should land exactly once, and not be repeated at the end because it sounds like an ending.

## 3. Shape

- **Opening:** committed to something concrete in the first paragraph, or throat-clearing? Abstract framing before the material is a finding.
- **Ending:** does it leave the harder underlying problem, the thing we cannot yet know, or a question genuinely left open? A tidy recap is a finding. So is a call to action inside the argument.
- **Structure:** has the piece turned itself into a framework, a checklist or a set of lessons? Sometimes the argument is the structure.
- **Formatting:** do the headers, bullets and lists earn it, or are they organising something that reads better as prose?
- **Format fit:** essay, practice note, GDS blog, institutional paper, short professional format. Has one been flattened into another? An essay ending imported into an email, essay apparatus in a LinkedIn post.

## 4. Line

Run `scripts/check.py` first, then read for what it cannot see.

- Paragraphs as units of developed thought, or units of pacing? Single-sentence paragraphs stacked for punch?
- Sentence length varying with the thought, or alternating on purpose? Connected reasoning chopped into short sentences for pace?
- Contraction register mixed, or contracted throughout to sound conversational?
- Any elegant variation, terms swapped to avoid repetition? Use the name again.
- Any manufactured third item in a list, there because the sentence sounded rhetorically complete?
- Tic words from section 3F clustering. One per section is voice.
- Credit present and linked wherever a framing came from someone?
- Is there padding? Shorter and accurate beats longer and fluffy.

## 5. The litmus test

> Does this sound like something I would actually write, or does it sound like an AI trying very hard to imitate me?

Answer it honestly, including about your own draft. If it feels forced, name the passage where the forcing shows.

## Reporting

Lead with the most serious finding. If the argument is thin, say that first and plainly, and do not bury it under a list of line edits. Give the evidence: quote the passage.

Separate what you changed from what you are suggesting. Suggestions get a reason. And if the piece is good, say so briefly and stop, rather than manufacturing findings to look thorough.
