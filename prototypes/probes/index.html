Experimentation as probes

Seven directions for one small illustration in Tasting the organisational treacle. A working page, not yet in the essay. Each one works as a still; the controls are there to play with.

You start to work with the materials of the institution itself, discovering how they had been arranged around the existing service and where you might rearrange them to make something different possible.

The illustration goes here

This is exactly where the value of experimentation lies. Discovering what an institution is made of, where its arrangements can be challenged and what might become possible if we started designing with those materials rather than treating them as fixed conditions around our work.

APull one thread

We learn what a system is made of by trying to change something within it.

What it shows

An institution as a cloth woven from different materials, each with its own texture, none of them underneath the others. Pull one thread and the ones it crosses pucker, so the connections only show when something moves. Legislation bends least.

What it loses

A weave is regular and an institution isn't. It reads as everything touching everything in a tidy grid, and it can suggest the thread comes out cleanly. It says little about room to rearrange, only about connection.

As a still for the essay

Two ways to show the movement without the slider. Arrows on the pulled cloth, or the cloth before and after with the arrows on the second. Both follow the pull above.

BRebalance the mobile

Sometimes a different service becomes possible by changing the relationships between existing materials.

What it shows

The materials hang in a balance that produces the current service. A small weight shifts the free arms, the fixed joint stays put, and the change gets taken up somewhere else, where it makes another arrangement possible. Constructive, and honest that not everything swings.

What it loses

Balance implies the system settles, and that one weight always finds a new equilibrium. It's the most mechanical of the three, and a mobile is a sculpture someone made on purpose, which institutions mostly aren't.

CTaste the treacle

Experiments help us discover what gets stuck, and where there may be room to move.

What it shows

The institution as texture you only feel by moving through it. The marks bunch where it's thick, so the drawing reads as slowing down. Neat rings are constraints that protect something, and the probe goes round them. Tangles are habit, and it pushes through them, slowly, sometimes turning a loop.

What it loses

It doesn't name any materials, so it leans on the essay to say what the friction is. It's about resistance more than rearrangement, and the difference between a ring and a tangle is a visual hint a reader might not take.

DReach beneath the service

A small experiment on the surface pulls you into the arrangements behind a service.

What it shows

The service as a surface, with its materials lying underneath, out of sight. The experiment reaches through and lifts one thread, the threads tied to it come up too, and the arrangement shows around the place it pulled. It puts A's threads into the layers of the place drawing, and legislation, deepest, moves least.

What it loses

Putting materials beneath the service can suggest they're hidden on purpose, or that deeper means more important, and the order of the layers is a choice. The reveal works best moving, so as a still it leans on the arrows.

ERearrange the pieces

Sometimes a different service becomes possible by moving what's already there.

What it shows

Materials as pieces fitted together, a row for each. As things stand the experiment can't find a way through. Swap two pieces in two rows and the gaps line up into a route, while legislation stays as it was and already had a gap in the right place. It shows designing with the materials as well as finding them.

What it loses

Pieces have clean edges and slide freely, and institutions don't. It can make rearranging look like a puzzle with one answer, and it says little about how the experiment found out which pieces to move.

FPush through the layers

An experiment learns where an institution resists by pushing into it.

What it shows

C turned on its side, with the materials named. The probe pushes down and drags each layer with it, more where it's sticky, and the marks on the rod bunch where it slowed. It stops at legislation, which dents and holds, so a legitimate constraint reads differently from friction.

What it loses

Depth reads as hierarchy again, and a push says more about resistance than rearrangement. One straight line is less playful than C's wandering path, and it's close to a diagram.

GBring it into view

A small intervention brings existing arrangements into view. The connections between materials matter as much as the materials themselves.

What it shows

The whole system is a circle, and its top edge is the visible service. The experiment reaches through and opens a window onto one slice of it. Inside, it touches one material, the relationships draw out one at a time, and the materials at their ends come into view, passing over and under each other where they cross. Outside the window, hatching marks what hasn't been looked at yet, with more materials faintly underneath, so the slice reads as a slice.

What it loses

A circle makes the system look bounded and knowable, which it isn't, and five materials all joined to each other is tidier than any real institution. The order things appear in is a choice. It sits close to a diagram, and leans on the hatching and textures to stay an illustration.

How they compare

Does it show something the prose doesn't? C does most clearly. The paragraph after the slot says what an institution is made of, and C gives the feel of moving through it instead. A shows connection, which the prose implies but doesn't show. B shows rearrangement, which the next paragraph names.

Materials and their relationships. A is strongest, with five named materials and visible knock-on effects. B names four and shows that they differ in how freely they move. C names none.

Experimentation as active inquiry. All three have the experiment doing something. In A it pulls, in B it weighs, in C it travels. C is the only one where the probe learns by moving rather than by acting once.

Legitimate constraints as well as friction. B and C both build this in, B with the fixed joint and C with rings against tangles. A only hints at it, through the stiffer legislation threads.

Next to the navigation model. C complements it best, since it's the treacle itself rather than another structure. A sits closest to the Materials prototype, and its threads are not far from those arcs. B introduces a second mechanical object, which competes a little.

On a phone. All three shrink to about a quarter of their size, and the small labels go below reading size. C loses nothing, because it never relied on words. B still reads as a mobile tipping. A turns into a fine grid, and without its labels it's the hardest to read at that size.

D, E and F. These came after A, B and C, from a sketch of an experiment that looks small on the surface and exposes much more underneath. D does that most directly, and mixes A's threads with the layers, so it keeps A's named materials and adds the sense of reaching in. E is the only one that shows designing with the materials, which is the essay's next step. F is C with names, clearer and less distinctive.

G. The closest to the sketch of an experiment that looks small on the surface and exposes much more underneath. It names the materials like A, keeps the surface from D, and is the only one where the relationships are as much the subject as the materials. It's also the only one that shows the slice as a slice, with the rest of the system hatched around it. It works as a still at the end, or partway, with the rest still dotted.

Worth the space? C is the most distinctive and the most CIVICWORKS. A is the clearest to a first-time reader. B is the most playful but the least likely to survive as a still.
