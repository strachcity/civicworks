Abstract 
Neighbourhoods are increasingly positioned as an effective scale for tackling technological, 
social, and ecological challenges. Yet despite decades of research and policy attention, urban 
development has struggled to deliver neighbourhoods capable of meeting the challenges of the 
twenty-first century. Mission-oriented approaches highlight the role of transition intermediaries 
in bridging capability deficits within public organisations, but little is known about the practices  
through which they design, adapt, and negotiate missions at the neighbourhood scale. This study 
addresses this gap by examining the practices of systemic, niche, and process intermediaries 
Using a grounded theory methodology, it draws on 15 semi-structured interviews: four with 
stakeholders from mission-oriented neighbourhood initiatives in the UK, Belgium, Canada, and 
Australia, and 1 1 with wider expert practitioners, alongside supporting document analysis.
The findings identify an emerging typology of four dimensions of practice – dynamic stewardship, 
shaping, sensemaking, and framing – articulated through nine interrelated routines that 
reconfigure the socio-spatial dimensions of neighbourhoods. These practices foster civic 
capacity, embed experimentation in everyday infrastructures, curate reflexive forms of evidence, 
and construct narratives that mobilise collective purpose, while also facing tensions around 
participation, data, and narrative capture. By grounding mission-oriented innovation in the socio-
spatial realities of neighbourhoods, the study extends scholarship on intermediaries and public 
sector innovation. It shows how intermediary practices compensate for, but remain constrained by 
capability deficits, underscoring the need for institutional reform if mission neighbourhoods are to 
achieve systemic transformation.

1. Introduction 
Neighbourhoods are essential spaces for communities to thrive. Yet despite decades of research 
and policy attention, urban development has consistently fallen short of delivering socially and 
environmentally sustainable neighbourhoods capable of meeting the challenges of the twenty-
first century (Grillitsch, Coenen & Morgan, 2025; Pagh & Cook, 2023). Instead, neighbourhoods 
have been shaped through fragmented approaches, driven by competing assumptions about 
what they are, who they are for, and what they should do (Allen, 2018; Hemmersam, 2023; Talen, 
2017). This has left the production of the built environment in the hands of capital interests, 
outdated planning systems, and chance – fuelling chronic underinvestment, spatial degradation, 
and the systematic hollowing out of urban spaces (Gómez-Mont, 2021; Mattern, 2019; 
Neighbourhoods Commission, 2025).
Against this backdrop, a “third generation” of innovation policy has emerged. Mission-oriented 
(Mazzucato, 2018), challenge-driven (Coenen et al., 2015), and transformative innovation 
(Diercks et al., 2019) policy approaches have reframed societal challenges as drivers of systemic 
transformation, catalysing a wave of trans-local initiatives that experiment with bureaucracy, 
rethink citizen engagement, and create new forms of public good (Kattel et al., 2025; Schot 
& Steinmueller, 2018; Uyarra et al., 2025; Wanzenböck & Frenken, 2022). Within this agenda, 
mission neighbourhoods  are proposed as the scale at which technological, social, and ecological 
change converge – leveraging physical and social infrastructures, governance, and everyday life 
as levers of transformation (Pagh & Cook, 2023).
Realising this potential, however, depends on institutional capabilities that public organisations 
typically lack. Supranational and national innovation policy remains “spatially blind,” overlooking 
the intricate mix of social and physical relations that define neighbourhoods. A “missing middle” 
in innovation infrastructure paralyses coordination across levels of public and private institutions, 
while the absence of generative institutions limits sustained collaboration (Bellinson, 2020; 
Fastenrath et al., 2023; Kattel, 2022; Mazzucato, 2024; Sørensen & Torfing, 2022; Uyarra et al., 
2025). Scholars therefore highlight the role of transition intermediaries – systemic, niche, and 
process actors – in bridging these deficits by anchoring missions in place, mobilising legitimacy, 
and facilitating experimentation (Kivimaa et al., 2019; Sovacool et al., 2020; Von Wirth et al., 
2019).
Surprisingly, relatively little is known about the practices through which intermediaries 
design, adapt, and negotiate urban missions at neighbourhood scale (Beer, 2023; Ehnert 
et al., 2023; Flanagan, Uyarra & Wanzenböck, 2022; Mukhtar-Landgren et al., 2019; Pagh 
& Cook, 2023; Rossi et al., 2021; Von Wirth et al., 2019). Scholarship has typically framed 
intermediaries in abstract roles, functions, and capabilities, while empirical accounts of practice 
remain fragmented, contextual, and undertheorised (Ehnert et al., 2023; Fauth, De Moortel & 
Schuurman, 2024; Fastenrath et al., 2023).

This represents a significant knowledge gap: without a grounded understanding of 3

institutionalised, and diffused – or even reinforcing the very challenges they seek to address 
(Kanda, Magnusson & Hjelm, 2024; Kattel & Mazzucato, 2025). 
This study therefore asks: What are the practices of systemic, niche, and process 
intermediaries in designing, adapting, and negotiating urban missions at the neighbourhood 
scale?
To answer this question, the study uses a grounded theory methodology, drawing on 15 semi-
structured interviews: four with stakeholders from mission-oriented neighbourhood initiatives in 
the UK, Belgium, Canada, and Australia, and 1 1 with wider expert practitioners, supplemented by 
document analysis. This approach generated comparative empirical material, analysed inductively 
to develop theory from the ground up. As mission initiatives remain emergent, this study is 
explorative – providing a snapshot of practices and their tensions.
The findings identify four dimensions of urban mission practice articulated through nine 
interrelated routines: (1) Dynamic stewardship , orchestrating the “messy middle” through 
strategic agility, shifting altitudes, and relational trust; (2) Shaping , configuring infrastructures 
through participatory scaffolding and street-level prototyping; (3) Sensemaking , curating 
and translating evidence into insights; and (4) Framing , building vocabularies and narratives 
that mobilise collective purpose. Together, these practices reveal how systemic ambitions are 
translated into everyday routines, grounding mission-oriented innovation in the socio-spatial 
realities of neighbourhoods.
Building on this grounded theory model, the study develops an emerging typology of urban 
mission practice that interprets both their generative capacities and their tensions. In turn, this 
typology provides a framework for linking practice-based insights to broader accounts of dynamic 
capabilities and public sector innovation, showing how intermediary practices both compensate 
for and remain constrained by capability deficits. The study highlights that without institutional 
reform to address these deficits, mission neighbourhoods risk becoming symbolic experiments – 
celebrated in discourse but unable to reshape the systems they inhabit.
This study is structured as follows: section 2 develops the theoretical background, defining 
neighbourhoods, situating missions as third-generation innovation policy, and highlighting 
capability deficits and intermediary roles; section 3 outlines the grounded theory methodology, 
including data sampling, collection, and analysis; section 4 presents the findings, introducing 
the four practice dimensions before framing them within case vignettes; section 5 develops the 
discussion, advancing an emerging typology of urban mission practice and interrogating the 
epistemic and normative tensions of mission neighbourhoods; and section 6 concludes with 
contributions, limitations, and directions for future research. 
2. Theoretical background
2.1 What is a neighbourhood?
Over the past century, neighbourhoods have been continuously (re)configured as the ideal 
scale to tackle technological, social, and economic challenges (Hemmersam, 2023; Hill, 
2023; Pagh & Cook, 2023). Early 20th-century models such as Ebenezer Howard’s Garden 
City and Clarence Perry’s neighbourhood unit envisioned self-sufficient communities, linking 
urban form to collective land trusts and service catchments that made residents stakeholders 
in local governance (Howard, 1902; Perry, 1929). Post-war New Towns in the UK and Soviet 
microdistricts scaled up these ideas through state development corporations, centralising 
decisions and curtailing local discretion (Hemmersam, 2023). Contemporary approaches from 
New Urbanism – including eco-neighbourhoods, smart cities, and the 15-minute city – prioritise 
accessibility and environmental goals while recentering municipalities and neighbourhood 
associations in participatory planning, usually via consultation rather than long-term stewardship 
(Mattern, 2019; Moreno et al., 2015). Yet, the intricate mix of physical and social relations that 
constitute a neighbourhood have made it consistently difficult for researchers, policymakers, and 
planners to arrive at a single, universally accepted definition (Allen, 2018; Hemmersam, 2023; 
Keller, 1966; Pagh & Cook, 2023; Sullivan & Taylor, 2007). As a result, neighbourhoods have 
been both planned and understood through diverse, context-specific approaches shaped by 
differing assumptions about what neighbourhoods are, who they are for, and what they should do 
(Allen, 2018; Hemmersam, 2023; Talen, 2017).
Scholars typically categorise neighbourhood concepts into three categories: (1) spatial; (2) 
social; and (3) socio-spatial (Allen, 2018). The spatial perspective, derived from architecture and 
urban planning, focuses on physical boundaries, layout, and built form, emphasising functional 
coherence (Hillier, 2007; Talen, 2017). The social perspective, derived from social science, 
understands neighbourhoods as social constructs produced through interaction, perception, 
and shared meaning (Keller, 1966; Chaskin, 1997; Sullivan & Taylor, 2007). The socio-spatial 
perspective, grounded in Massey's relational conception of place, integrates these two realms by 
understanding neighbourhoods as dynamic products of the interplay between spatial structures 
and social processes (Allen, 2018; Massey, 2005).
Given these multifaceted definitions, research into neighbourhoods – and the practices that 
shape them – requires a clear conceptual framework (Allen, 2018; Sullivan & Taylor, 2007). While 
spatial and social perspectives offer valuable but partial insights, this study adopts a socio-spatial 
perspective as a productive lens for examining missions at the neighbourhood scale, defining 
neighbourhoods as “both the physical context in which people live and the social opportunities 
available” (Pagh & Cook, 2023).

2.2 Why mission neighbourhoods?
Despite a substantial body of literature positioning neighbourhoods as the ideal scale to tackle 
complex problems, contemporary urban development and policymaking has fallen short of 
delivering socially and environmentally sustainable neighbourhoods capable of meeting the 
challenges of the 21st century (Grillitsch, Coenen and Morgan, 2025; Institute for Government, 
2025; Local Trust, 2025; Moallemi et al., 2020; Neighbourhoods Commission, 2025; Pagh 
& Cook, 2023; Uyarra et al., 2025; Wanzenböck & Frenken, 2020). Instead, neoliberal and 
technocratic approaches to urban planning and policy – such as cost–benefit evaluation 
frameworks, or efficiency-oriented ‘smart city’ agendas – have frequently left the production of 
the built environment in the hands of capital interests, outdated planning systems, and chance 
– fuelling chronic underinvestment, spatial degradation, and the systematic hollowing out of 
urban spaces (Gómez-Mont, 2021; Neighbourhoods Commission, 2025; Pagh & Cook, 2023; 
Mattern, 2019). These reductionist approaches – privileging efficiency and economic growth 
over complexity and local relevance – have stripped places of their capacity to mobilise the 
neighbourhood scale for transformative change (Bellinson, 2022; Garza & Garza, 2024; Institute 
for Government, 2025; Mazzucato & Ryan-Collins, 2022; Neighbourhoods Commission, 2025; 
Pagh & Cook, 2023; Wanzenböck & Frenken, 2020).
Since the mid-2010s, however, there has been a rise in "third generation" approaches – ranging 
from mission-oriented innovation policy (Mazzucato, 2018), challenge-driven policy (Coenen et 
al., 2015), and transformative innovation policy (Diercks et al., 2019; Schot & Steinmueller, 2018). 
Under the umbrella of "missions," these approaches aim to drive systemic transitions to address 
global challenges such as climate change, social inequality, and food security (Fastenrath 
et al., 2023; Hill, 2022; Kattel & Mazzucato, 2025; Schot & Steinmueller, 2018; Uyarra et 
al., 2025; Wanzenböck & Frenken, 2020). While missions have largely been framed within 
regional innovation systems and sustainability transitions, their uptake – particularly in Europe 
– has catalysed a wave of trans-local initiatives and public organisations driving collaborative 
engagement and bottom-up experimentation (Bellinson, 2022; Kattel et al., 2025; Uyarra et al., 
2025; Wanzenböck & Frenken, 2022), including the EU Mission for 100 Climate-Neutral and 
Smart Cities, the OECD Mission Lab, DEAL, UNDP’s M4EG Facility, and C40 Cities (Kattel et 
al., 2025). Place-based framing has become central to translating broad missions into context-
specific goals, strategies, and actions (Ali, Brazell, Somerville, & Wyld, 2025; Bellinson, 2022; 
Fastenrath et al., 2023; Hill, 2022; Uyarra et al., 2025; Wanzenböck & Frenken, 2020).
Within this turn, scholars propose "mission neighbourhoods" (Neighbourhoods Commission, 
2025; Pagh & Cook, 2023) – or similarly "mission-oriented innovation districts" (Fastenrath 
et al., 2023) – as a way to direct innovation embedded within socio-technical structures to 
shape neighbourhoods as mechanisms for transformational change. In other words, positioning 
the neighbourhood as the ideal scale for systemic transformation by leveraging socio-spatial 
dimensions of the neighbourhood not as static features, but as active levers for change (Institute 
for Government, 2025; Kattel et al., 2025; Local Trust, 2025; Mazzucato, 2018, 2024; Moallemi 
et al., 2020; Wanzenböck & Frenken, 2020). In turn, this places attention on the innovation 
capabilities of the public sector and the governance arrangements that shape how mission 
neighbourhoods are formed (Mazzucato & Dibb, 2019; Pagh & Cook, 2023).

2.3 Capabilities for mission neighbourhoods
In the context of public sector organisations, recent scholarship highlights that public sector 
innovation capabilities are vital for addressing complex societal challenges (Kattel, 2022; 
Sørensen & Torfing, 2022; Wolfram, Borgström & Farrelly, 2019). Successfully forming mission 
neighbourhoods, for instance, depends significantly on the capacities and capabilities of public 
authorities, with scholars arguing that for missions to be transformative, governments must 
reconfigure bureaucracy, foster cross-sectoral collaboration, and enable participatory processes 
(Björk et al., 2022; Ehnert, 2023; Kattel & Mazzucato, 2025; Sovacool et al., 2020). To this end, 
Sørensen & Torfing (2022) conceptualise Three Orders of Public Innovation (Figure 2) as the 
enabling conditions to stabilise and scale these processes: first-order changes in policies and 
services, second-order innovations in collaborative processes, and third-order innovations in 
institutional designs.
Figure 2. How the three orders of innovation condition and transform each other.
Source: Adapted from Sørensen & Torfing, 2022
Kattel (2022) extends this perspective, focusing on the dynamic capabilities – the ability to 
integrate, build, and reconfigure competencies in response to changing environments – that 
public organisations must develop in order to enable new forms of public innovation. These 
capabilities are synthesised as routines of sensing (identifying problems and opportunities), 
seizing (mobilising networks and resources), and transforming (reconfiguring institutions and 
practices). Viewed together, Sørensen & Torfing’s three orders of innovation describe what kinds 
of change missions demand, while Kattel’s dynamic capabilities highlight how governments can 
generate and maintain those changes in practice (Kattel, 2022; Sørensen & Torfing, 2022).
At the neighbourhood scale, however, conditions and capabilities for public innovation face 
several governance deficits that limit their capacity to deliver missions (Bellinson, 2020; 
Fastenrath et al., 2023; Mazzucato, 2024; Sørensen & Torfing, 2022; Uyarra et al., 2025). 
National mission-oriented innovation policy remains “spatially blind,” overlooking the intricate 
mix of social and physical relations that define neighbourhoods – reflecting a lack of sensing  
(Fastenrath et al., 2023; Uyarra et al., 2025). A “missing middle” in innovation infrastructure 
paralyses coordination across levels of public and private institutions – reflecting a lack of 
seizing (Bellinson, 2020; Mazzucato, 2024). While the absence of “generative institutions” 
(Ansell & Torfing, 2021) weakens transforming capabilities by limiting sustained cross-sectoral 
collaboration (Fastenrath et al., 2023; Uyarra et al., 2025). Collectively, these deficits constrain 
both democratic legitimacy and operational capacity (Wanzenböck & Frenken, 2020; Kalliomäki, 
Oinas, & Salo, 2023).
2.4 Bridging the missing middle
In response, scholarship in sustainability transitions, urban innovation, and public sector 
innovation highlights the role of transition intermediaries in bridging the “missing middle” of urban 
mission governance (Ehnert, 2023; Fastenrath et al., 2023; Kanda et al., 2020; Kivimaa et al., 
2019; Mazzucato, 2024; Schot & Steinmueller, 2018; Sovacool et al., 2020; Uyarra et al., 2025; 
Von Wirth et al., 2019). Scholars argue that intermediaries “anchor” missions in their specific 
spatial, social, and economic contexts – bridging social innovation niches and established 
regimes through long-term system orchestration and support that builds civic capacity (Bulkeley 
et al., 2017; Fauth, De Moortel & Schuurman, 2024; Fastenrath et al., 2023; Hurmelinna-
Laukkanen, Möller & Nätti, 2022; Kalliomäki, Oinas, & Salo, 2023; Kivimaa et al., 2019; Sovacool 
et al., 2020; Uyarra et al., 2025; Von Wirth et al., 2019). Kanda et al. (2020) locate this anchoring 
across system levels: (1) in-between entities in a network; (2) in-between networks of entities; 
and (3) in-between actors, networks, and institutions – pinpointing where intermediaries 
compensate for weak dynamic capabilities (Bellinson, 2020; Ehnert, 2023; Fastenrath et al., 
2023; Mazzucato, 2024).
However, the literature varies widely in how it defines intermediaries – ranging from formal, 
recognised entities to emergent, informal actors, under public, private, or hybrid governance 
arrangements (Ehnert, 2023; Hurmelinna-Laukkanen, Möller & Nätti, 2022; Kanda et al., 2020; 
Kivimaa, 2014; Kivimaa et al., 2019; Sovacool et al., 2020). Table 1 synthesises this literature, 
providing a conceptual scaffolding of intermediary types, their position and their contributions to 
urban missions.
While the wider literature distinguishes five types, this study adopts three analytically useful 
archetypes for urban missions: systemic intermediaries coordinating cross-sectoral collaboration 
(seizing); niche intermediaries mobilising communities and local legitimacy (sensing); and process 
intermediaries facilitating place-based experimentation (transforming) (Ehnert, 2023; Sovacool et 
al., 2020).

2.5 Practices for mission neighbourhoods
Despite the substantial body of literature detailing the dynamic capabilities of public 
organisations and the roles and functions of transition intermediaries, relatively little empirical 
evidence exists on the practices – that is, the routines and behaviours of actors – in performing 
these roles and functions (Beer, 2023; Ehnert et al., 2023; Fastenrath et al., 2023; Flanagan, 
Uyarra & Wanzenböck, 2022; Kattel & Mazzucato, 2025; Mukhtar-Landgren et al., 2019; Rossi 
et al., 2021; Uyarra et al., 2025; Von Wirth et al., 2019). This distinction is crucial: while roles and 
functions describe what intermediaries do in abstracted terms, practices capture the situated, 
everyday ways intermediaries routinise working methods, tools, and skills (Reckwitz, 2002; Teece, 
2022) – shaping whether missions become inclusive and lasting, or whether they reproduce 
existing governance logics and systemic issues.

Because missions challenge the epistemic foundations of urban development and policymaking, 
this lack of attention is critical (Kattel & Mazzucato, 2025). Without a comprehensive 
understanding of how practices unfold, they cannot be activated or institutionalised to influence 
the wider systemic change that urban missions seek to achieve (Teece, 2022). Instead, mission 
approaches risk struggling to make the epistemic turn in practice – that is, to fundamentally shift 
how knowledge, policy, and place are formed (Kattel & Mazzucato, 2025). Subsequently, missions 
at the urban scale risk remaining abstract aspirations, constrained to business-as-usual practice 
and incremental improvements, rather than becoming routinised and scalable ways of organising 
systemic change (Björk et al., 2022; Kanda, Magnusson & Hjelm, 2024; Kattel & Mazzucato, 
2025).
Few scholarly and practice-based contributions address this gap, but those that exist 
remain fragmented and inconsistent in focus. Von Wirth et al. (2019) offer one of the few 
scholarly conceptualisations of intermediary practice, identifying six strategies through which 
intermediaries seek to institutionalise routines contributing to mission-oriented change. Their 
emphasis, however, lies on diffusion – how experimental practices travel and influence broader 
systems – rather than on the situated, everyday practices themselves (Table 3). While valuable 
as a partial account, this contribution leaves open questions about how intermediaries design, 
coordinate, cooperate, or compete with other actors, and how these practices influence policy 
processes in socio-spatial contexts (Von Wirth et al., 2019; Tobin, Farstad & Tosun, 2023).

Alongside these limited scholarly contributions, a growing set of practice-based accounts have 
emerged that explore how mission practices unfold in context. Most prominently, Vinnova’s 
Mission Design Framework applies strategic design to frame a set of design-led practices: 
generating diverse forms of analysis and knowledge through interaction and dialogue, 
synthesising tangible concepts, testing and refining prototypes, and generating insights to shape 
the design and delivery of mission initiatives (Hill, 2023). In this context, strategic design is 
leveraged as a practical lens to bring together disparate disciplines and perspectives – enabling 
a more holistic understanding of how missions are practiced (Hill, 2023; Mazzucato, 2024; 
Pagh & Cook, 2023). Hill & Melander (2025) synthesise these contributions into four design 
capabilities for public purpose: integration (discovering and describing the needs of people 
and place in systemic and synthetic fashion), invention (embracing uncertainty and cultural 
imagination to sketch alternative trajectories), prototyping (building and testing models in public 
as an iterative, participatory process), and stewardship (ensuring ideas evolve and embed through 
learning in context).
These accounts advance beyond abstract functions but remain undertheorised and highly 
contextual. They depend on the specific conditions that enabled them to unfold, and are often 
framed through the language of design “magic” that resists diffusion and generalisation (Dorst, 
201 1; Manzini, 2015). This underscores the need for an integrated account of intermediary 
practice – explaining how practices are configured, connected, and routinised in contested 
neighbourhood contexts (Kattel & Mazzucato, 2025; Pagh & Cook, 2023).
2.6 Synthesis and gaps
Although recent scholarship has expanded understanding of mission-oriented policymaking at 
the neighbourhood scale (Fauth, De Moortel & Schuurman, 2024; Fastenrath et al., 2023) and 
highlighted the role of transition intermediaries in addressing capability deficits (Kivimaa et al., 
2019), empirical insight into intermediary practice remains limited and fragmented (Beer, 2023; 
Ehnert et al., 2023; Flanagan, Uyarra & Wanzenböck, 2022; Mukhtar-Landgren et al., 2019; Pagh 
& Cook, 2023; Rossi et al., 2021; Uyarra et al., 2025; Von Wirth et al., 2019). Where contributions 
address practice, they either emphasise diffusion (e.g., Von Wirth et al., 2019) or provide rich, 
context-bound design accounts (e.g., Hill, 2023; Hill & Melander, 2025) without explaining how 
practices unfold as everyday routines and behaviours within socio-spatial settings (Ehnert et al., 
2023; Fauth, De Moortel & Schuurman, 2024; Fastenrath et al., 2023).
This underscores the need for deeper empirical evidence of how urban missions are designed, 
adapted, and negotiated at the neighbourhood scale (Fastenrath et al., 2023; Kanda et al., 
2020; Pagh & Cook, 2023; Uyarra et al., 2025). Without such evidence, mission practices risk 
remaining un-operationalised and un-institutionalised—or even reinforcing existing challenges 
(Kanda, Magnusson & Hjelm, 2024; Kattel & Mazzucato, 2025). Addressing this gap requires 
studying the everyday routines and behaviours through which urban mission ambitions are 
translated into situated practices.

This study therefore asks:
RQ: What are the practices of systemic, niche, and process intermediaries in the design, 
adaptation, and negotiation of urban missions at the neighbourhood scale?
By examining these practices, the study moves beyond abstract roles and functions to detail the 
everyday routines of transition intermediaries and proposes an emerging framework illuminating 
how practices are configured and interrelated in forming mission neighbourhoods.
3. Methodology
This study examines the practices of systemic, niche, and process intermediaries in the design, 
adaptation, and negotiation of urban missions at the neighbourhood scale. The objective is to 
develop a grounded-theory framework of these practices and their interdependencies, linking 
orders of innovation, dynamic capabilities, and intermediary functions to the everyday routines 
through which mission neighbourhoods take shape.
Given the limited empirical evidence in this area (Beer, 2023; Ehnert et al., 2023; Flanagan, 
Uyarra & Wanzenböck, 2022; Mukhtar-Landgren et al., 2019; Pagh & Cook, 2023; Rossi et 
al., 2021; Uyarra et al., 2025; von Wirth et al., 2019), and the dynamic, complex, and context-
dependent nature of practices (Reckwitz, 2002), this research adopts an exploratory grounded 
theory approach as a "creative, yet systematic" lens (Volmar & Eisenhardt, 2020). 
Grounded theory is suited to uncovering social processes, contextual conditions, and 
consequences – supporting concept development that explains how urban missions 
are designed, adapted, and negotiated (Corley & Gioia, 2004; Glaser & Strauss, 1999). 
Acknowledging the difficulties in ensuring analytical rigour in grounded theory (emphasised by 
Charmaz, 2006), this study adopts the Gioia methodology as a structured framework for iterative 
data collection and theory development (Corley & Gioia, 2004).
Four qualitative case studies were constructed using document analysis and semi-structured 
interviews, balancing openness of discovery with sufficient structure for concept and category 
building (Corley & Gioia, 2004). A multiple case design provided a complementary strategy for 
situating and studying variation across contexts, enabling iterative engagement with the field – 
generating richer insights into the ‘how’ of dynamic and complex practices beyond abstracted 
capabilities and functions (Stewart, 201 1; Eisenhardt, 1989; Gehman et al., 2017; Yazan, 2015; 
Volmar & Eisenhardt, 2020).
3.1 Data sampling and case selection
Key to methodological rigour in multiple-case research is the process of case selection (Von 
Wirth et al., 2019). Following Eisenhardt’s (1989) guidance on theory-building from case 
research, this study combines purposive and theoretical sampling (Lincoln & Guba, 1985; Glaser 
& Strauss, 1999), enabling the generation of rich insights into urban mission practices.
Purposive sampling was used to identify information-rich cases of urban missions relevant to the 
research question. 252 place-based initiatives were identified from the Doughnut Economics 
Action Lab (DEAL) community database and the EU's Climate-Neutral and Smart Cities 
initiative. Cases were screened against two criteria: (1) explicitly neighbourhood scale; and (2) 
facilitated by systemic, niche, or process intermediaries evidencing mission-oriented activity (see 
Section 2.4). Thirty cases met both criteria. Twenty-six were excluded due to insufficient public 
documentation or lack of access to key stakeholders. Four cases – in the UK, Belgium, Canada, 
and Australia – were retained for their potential to illuminate the research question.

As data collection and analysis progressed, theoretical sampling – "a hallmark of grounded 
theory" (Eisenhardt, 1989) – was employed to identify additional participants in response to 
emerging theory from ongoing analysis and iterative comparison (Glaser & Strauss, 1999). 
Snowball sampling complemented this, with interviewees recommending further informants 
recognised as experts in urban missions and place-based transformation.
Sampling continued as an iterative process of collecting and analysing data, and identifying new 
cases and participants in order to seek theoretical saturation – the point at which no new themes 
or insights were emerging from additional data (Eisenhardt, 1989; Glaser & Strauss, 1999; 
Small, 2009). The final dataset comprises four cases (each with a key stakeholder interview plus 
documentary analysis) and eleven expert interviews, providing depth within cases and cross-
context breadth.
3.2 Data collection
Data was collected using three complementary techniques: (1) document analysis; (2) semi-
structured case interviews; and (3) semi-structured expert stakeholder interviews. Case 
interviews formed the core empirical material, while documents and expert interviews 
triangulated and extended emerging theory (Corley & Gioia, 2004). 
Documentation selection:  Three types of documents were collected: (1) case reports detailing 
goals, activities, and contexts; (2) methodological texts authored by intermediaries, reflecting 
distinctive mission-oriented approaches; and (3) evaluation reports assessing processes and 
outcomes. Table 4 lists all documents analysed.
Fifteen semi-structured interviews were conducted between June and August 2025: four one-
to-one case interviews and eleven expert interviews. All interviews followed the same protocol, 
were remote, recorded, and automatically transcribed. Case participants were identified via 
the document review and selected for direct involvement with transition intermediaries leading 
urban missions. Additional participants were identified via the snowballing technique as 
‘expert’ practitioners, including policy leaders and representatives of place-based organisations 
supporting innovation and transformation. Table 5 summarises roles and characteristics.
Semi-structure interview protocol: The interview protocol pursued two objectives: (1) 
constructing rich, contextualised accounts of each mission, and (2) practice identification – 
asking “how” questions related to intermediary functions (see Section 2.4), while leaving space 
for novel practices to emerge. A pilot interview refined the protocol for depth and clarity (Yazan, 
2015). As analysis progressed, questions were adapted through constant comparison to probe 
emergent concepts (Corley & Gioia, 2004; Small, 2009).

3.3 Data analysis
The Gioia Methodology was employed to bring analytical rigour to grounded theory’s emphasis 
on developing concepts from the field, offering a structured process for interpreting qualitative 
data (Corley & Gioia, 2004). Following Corley and Gioia’s guidance, the analysis proceeded in 
three stages:
1. First-order coding: Key phrases and terms were manually coded (via Mural) to identify 
commonalities and recurring patterns. Case interviews were coded first, followed by expert 
interviews, which triangulated and extended emerging theory. This yielded 84 primary (case 
interviews) and 86 secondary (expert interviews) open codes (170 in total). These were 
grouped into 60 first-order concepts, preserving participants’ language to retain the lived 
meaning of their practices (Corley & Gioia, 2004).
2. Second-order coding:  First-order concepts were then analysed to identify relationships 
among categories, forming thematic clusters. This axial coding stage moved the analysis 
from description to explanation, producing nine second-order themes representing distinct 
urban mission practices.
3. Aggregate dimensions:  Second-order themes were synthesised into four aggregate 
dimensions capturing the underlying structure of the data. These consolidated multiple 
practices into broader conceptual categories. At this stage, the data structure did not depict 
relationships among themes, but balanced participants’ embedded views in first-order 
concepts with the analytical abstraction needed as the foundation for a grounded-theory 
model of relationships, interdependencies, and feedback loops.
This process was iterative rather than linear, aiming to achieve theoretical saturation. In line 
with the Gioia Methodology, the analysis is presented as a visual data structure (Figure 3), 
providing a systematic and transparent representation of how raw data progressed into first-order 
concepts, second-order themes, and aggregate dimensions (Gioia, Corley & Hamilton, 2013). The 
grounded theory model that builds on this structure is presented in the findings (Figure 4), where 
relationships and feedback dynamics between practices are made explicit.

As shown in Figure 4, four aggregate dimensions structure the model: (1) dynamic

4.2 Aggregate dimensions
Dynamic stewardship
Dynamic stewardship emerged as a central set of practices for orchestrating the “messy 
middle” (Participant I) between central and local governments, institutions, businesses, and 
neighbourhoods – aligning portfolios of initiatives, “shifting altitudes” between system levels, and 
fostering deeply trusting relationships that sustain momentum. Rather than assuming control, 
participants cast themselves as enablers who create conditions for collective action. This was 
described as a two-way interface: translating systemic missions into local action while elevating 
neighbourhood learning into strategy. As one participant put it, it is like “trying to sail a boat 
whilst building the boat” (Participant A) – steering towards a destination while continuously 
shaping the means to get there. 
Dynamic stewardship is made up of three interrelated practices:
Strategic agility:  Participants called for “agility in your strategy” (Participant E, F) when 
setting transition roadmaps for urban missions. These were conceived as outcome-oriented 
coordination tools – structured enough to align collective effort yet flexible to shifting 
politics, funding, and community sentiment. Participants stressed the need for a “test 
and learn” ethos, combining small-scale experiments with a “scope or die” (Participant C) 
approach to be clear about the scale of influence and ambition from the outset. 
One example was a bereavement café, discontinued after low participation despite its clear 
purpose, which underscored the need to adjust initiatives in real time (Participant H). Agility 
was less about speed and more about legitimacy. Demonstrating that missions could adapt 
without losing credibility with funders or ministers. For several participants, this paradox – 
being ambitious yet responsive – was not a side-effect but the very work of stewardship ; 
the means by which missions “move the needle” (Participant M) across system layers.
Shifting altitudes: Key to stewardship  was "shifting altitudes" (Participant B) – the ability 
to move fluidly between layers of understanding and action, from the "meta" (systemic, 
policy, and networked perspectives) to the "matter" (street-level, lived experience). This 
was less a technical skill than a two-way practice: translating lived experience upwards to 
influence decision-makers, while translating systemic shifts downwards to provide enabling 
conditions for local action. As one participant put it, stewardship  meant “making the system 
legible in a human way… seeing the interactions of lots of different policy areas… (and) 
making the dialogue go both ways across these different altitudes” (Participant N).
Participants stressed that without this movement, missions risked becoming detached from 
lived experience or unintelligible to policymakers. The difficulty was avoiding simplifications 
that downplayed the scale of reform. Bridging this gap required framing practices and 
developing nuanced “language architectures” (Practice H) that carried complexity without 
paralysing action. In this way, shifting altitudes sustains coherence across system layers 
while still rooting missions in place.
Relational stewardship: Trust emerged as the foundation of stewardship , with 
participants emphasising that “moving at the speed of trust” (Participant N), investing time, 
care, and authenticity in relationships – even when this conflicted with efficiency logics – 
was essential for urban missions. What distinguished this practice was a commitment to 
deep generosity, “rejecting the bare minimum” (Participant A) of transactional engagement 
in favour of deliberate, patient investment in people and places. Generosity was expressed 
through everyday acts of authenticity: commissioning local caterers, sharing meals, caring 
for the spaces where people gathered, and listening with genuine openness (Participants 
B, E, H, I, K).
Participants acknowledged the tension between slow trust-building and the systemic push 
for “getting things done,” yet argued that “friction is kind of the point” (Participant A), as it 
signals attempts to build something “dissonant with broader logics” (Participant C, O). A key 
tactic was “bringing the system into the room” (Participant B), convening “coalitions of the 
willing” (Participant F) across policymakers, academics, entrepreneurs, and communities to 
create durable infrastructures of trust. The result was what one participant called a “legacy 
of relationships” (Participant N) that anchored missions in neighbourhoods.
As a set, participants described these practices as the coordinating function of mission 
neighbourhoods. By weaving strategic agility, altitude-shifting, and relational trust, they 
created conditions to align diverse actors, adapted to shifting circumstances, and connected 
learning across systemic and lived realities in the "messy middle." Though never seamless, for 
intermediaries, stewardship was strategic: it sustained trust and enabled coordination under 
uncertainty.
Shaping
Shaping emerged as a set of practices for constructing the spatial and social architectures 
of neighbourhoods, with several participants describing their organisations as “community 
infrastructure companies” (Participants F, G, H, I, O) – providing accessible spaces, fostering 
collaboration, and experimenting with participatory governance. These practices were seen as 
building the civic capacities for experimentation and collective action, turning missions from 
abstract aspirations into everyday life. Within the grounded theory model, shaping functions as 
an iterative cycle: supported by stewardship , narrated through framing , and continually adapted 
through sensemaking from evolving evidence and insight.

Shaping is made up of two interrelated practices:
Participatory scaffolding:  Participants described this as creating the conditions for 
meaningful participation by “scaffolding social infrastructure” (Participants J, O). Rather 
than one-off consultations, scaffolding operated across three layers. Spatially, it meant 
constructing neutral “third spaces” (Participant F) where communities, practitioners, and 
officials could meet as equals – as with Citizen Hub, based in a central historic building in 
St Neots to signal community ownership (Participant H). Governance scaffolds set inclusive 
guardrails, such as Leuven 2030’s “quadruple helix” board, where citizens, companies, 
knowledge institutions, and the city share power and distribute influence transparently 
(Participant D). Socially, scaffolding built on trust from relational stewardship  to establish 
roles and expectations that made participation purposeful. 
While participants emphasised that “communities are the genuine experts of their 
own places and needs” (Participant I), others warned that over-reliance risked shifting 
responsibility away from institutions and reducing participation to tokenism or burden  
(Participants C, O). This tension was managed by framing engagement to guide 
involvement without overwhelming capacity – or, as one participant put it, by “going a mile 
deep and an inch wide” (Participant I).
Street-level prototyping: Prototyping was described as “learning our way through” 
(Participant D), turning abstract mission ideas into tangible experiments that test 
assumptions and reduce risk. These were artefacts, spaces, forms of governance and 
institutional prototypes. Participants spoke of “small bets before big bets” (Participant 
D), using low-risk experiments such as piloting a participatory grant-making app with 
a small sample of citizens to build confidence and direction before launching the idea. 
Prototyping depended on specific socio-spatial settings, where initiatives could be crafted 
with citizens, practitioners, and policymakers – enabling people to “walk around, feel it, and 
see it” (Participant B). Co-design sessions, street pilots, and civic assemblies were cited as 
methods that allowed stakeholders to “see themselves in the process” (Participant H), with 
their input visibly shaping outcomes
The cost of not prototyping was described as “creating more problems down the line” 
(Participant O): no clear signals, weak confidence in direction, and higher risk of costly 
failures at scale (Participants O, C). Leuven 2030’s 86 breakthrough projects embody this 
approach, each explicitly framed as “pilots to generate evidence, upscale, and accelerate 
further efforts” (Participant D). In this way, prototyping grounded missions in lived realities, 
making ambition tangible while managing uncertainty.
Together, these practices describe shaping  as the socio-spatial grounding of mission 
neighbourhoods. By scaffolding participation and prototyping at street level, abstract ambitions 
are translated into infrastructures, lived experiences, and collective experiments. Shaping makes 
missions credible and tangible, balancing empowerment with responsibility and embedding 
change in everyday community life. Within the grounded theory model, shaping  explains how 
missions take form in place – turning aspiration into practice through civic capacities that can be 
tested, adapted, and scaled.
Sensemaking
Sensemaking emerged as a crucial mechanism for understanding problems and contexts in 
neighbourhoods, moving beyond “how change is happening” to showing how it gets “into the 
body of a community” (Participant C). Participants stressed this was more than collecting data: 
it meant interrogating assumptions, tuning into lived experience, and situating findings within 
broader systemic perspectives. As one participant put it, it requires “really understanding the 
problem we're trying to solve… in all kinds of different ways” (Participant C). Within the grounded 
theory model, sensemaking  operates in continuous feedback loops with other practices – guiding 
shaping by generating evidence for experimentation, informing stewardship  by articulating 
changing conditions, and supporting framing by surfacing patterns and causal stories.
Sensemaking is made up of two parallel, interdependent practices:
Curating evidence: This meant proactively defining what data to collect, how, and why, 
“balancing the need for quantitative metrics with rich qualitative insights, and continually 
adapting these choices as urban missions evolve” (Participant O). Because missions 
generate vast amounts of information – from prototypes, assemblies, or surveys – 
participants warned of becoming lost in a “desert of data” (Participant D) unless evidence 
was deliberately curated. The skill lay in aligning evidence to evolving outcomes so it stayed 
context-specific and actionable.
Participants stressed the need to “switch between evidence” (Participant C): using lagging 
indicators such as annual CO₂ inventories to show systemic change, and leading indicators 
such as early qualitative signals from pilots to capture direction of travel (Participants C, 
D, O). These choices had to be revisited as conditions shifted, ensuring evidence evolved 
with context rather than being reduced to static reports. This agility was seen as essential 
for keeping missions grounded – avoiding paralysis from irrelevant data while generating 
signals that demonstrated progress, attracted funding, and influenced policy.
Translating evidence into insight: Participants emphasised that blending diverse 
forms of evidence – from statistical indicators, spatial data, and policy reviews, to lived 
experiences, civic assemblies, and local histories – in ways that surfaced patterns, 
hypothesised causal relationships, and identified leverage points was essential. Participants 
stressed this was more than listening: it required “active interpretation” (Participant J) so 
evidence could be mobilised across system layers while retaining contextual richness.

Framing
Framing emerged as a communicative set of practices that participants described as the 
“connective tissue” (Participant J) of urban missions, constructing shared vocabularies, crafting 
visions, and adapting narratives so they could travel across system layers without losing their 
grounding in place. Participants emphasised this meant “adapting complex information and 
visions that resonate with different audiences and contexts” (Participant B), making missions 
understandable to communities, legitimate for policymakers, and compelling to funders and 
investors. These practices demand trust, attentiveness, and deliberate adaptation, and within the 
grounded theory model they are inseparable from other dimensions: with stewardship  fostering 
language between system altitudes, shaping  offering tangible anchors for storytelling, and 
sensemaking generating the evidence that underpins narratives. 
Framing is made up of two nuanced practices:
Building language architectures: Participants highlighted the need to create shared 
vocabularies (or “lexicons”) as tools for both connection and disruption – grounding 
conversations in the history, identity, and lived experience of a place while unsettling 
business-as-usual framings. One participant explained, “ you have to speak the language 
of the organisation you’re talking to… otherwise you can’t even start the conversation ” 
(Participant B). This meant tuning into the words and references used across different 
“altitudes” and deliberately crafting metaphors, anecdotes, or other devices that resonated 
across audiences – framing what could be seen, understood, and acted on. 
For example, Regen Melbourne reframed the Birrarung River – widely seen as polluted 
and unswimmable – as the city’s “lifeblood” (Participant B), a metaphor that provoked 
citizens and institutions to imagine its restoration as central to the city’s future. Participants 
stressed that such reframing makes complex systems legible whilst also challenging 
assumptions about what change is required. Without these shared vocabularies, missions 
risked fragmenting as actors interpreted problems differently, weakening collective 
momentum and obscuring pathways for change.
Developing narratives:  Narrative development was described as a crucial practice for 
making sense of problems and imagining change through compelling visions and stories. 
Participants emphasised that effective narratives had to resonate – being memorable and 
repeatable in everyday conversation so they could spread through community touchpoints 
without formal marketing. This was described as “showing possibilities” rather than “telling 
and preaching” (Participant G), creating ownership and momentum that travelled organically 
– “from barbershops to taxi drivers” (Participant H).  
Narratives were also seen as dynamic, continually adapted as the work evolves” to push 
ideas into more “transformative” forms (Participant A). Participants explained that effective 
narratives collapsed systemic challenges into relatable lived experiences, while elevating 
local stories into systemic visibility. At a strategic level, narrative work was described as 
“building a movement” (Participant O) – keeping initiatives open so anyone with interest 
could contribute, avoiding elite steering groups and broadening legitimacy. Importantly, 
participants stressed that narratives had to be built with  communities, not for  them 
(Participants H, I).
These practices illustrate the communicative thread of mission neighbourhoods. By building 
language architectures and developing narratives, missions become tangible, legible, and 
compelling across audiences and altitudes. This coherence enables local stories to anchor 
neighbourhood change while also travelling outward to mobilise broader movements.

Civic Square: Neighbourhood Public Square
Civic Square, a Community Interest Company in Birmingham, positions itself as a long-term 
steward of neighbourhood transition, seeking to unsettle prevailing economic and governance 
logics. At its centre is the Neighbourhood Public Square (NPS), a living prototype for moving land 
out of speculation and into civic stewardship, embedding practices of shaping , stewardship, and 
sensemaking directly in the fabric of place.
This ambition is made tangible through the Floating Front Room , a canal barge designed as a “no 
barrier to entry” gathering space where neighbours can drop in, share food, or join assemblies. 
Such visible infrastructures cultivate reciprocity and accountability, rejecting transactional “bare 
minimum” logics in favour of attentive hosting, locally sourced food, and open communication. 
These routines weave stewardship  and shaping into the everyday work of building trust, though 
they also generate tension with efficiency-driven funders who view such practices as slow or 
resource-intensive.
Street-level prototyping extends this approach. The NPS functions as a testbed for regenerative 
civic infrastructure – trialling collective retrofitting, bio-based materials, and circular systems. 
Rather than importing predefined “best practice,” Civic Square treats the site as an evolving 
experiment, likened by one participant to “sailing a boat whilst building the boat”. This orientation 
produces friction with conventional institutions, which expect certainty, but the organisation 
frames dissonance as intentional: modelling infrastructures for an alternative urban future.
These prototypes also act as sensemaking devices, with the “site as a classroom” generating 
collective learning and new skills for transition. Civic Square illustrates how shaping , stewardship, 
and sensemaking practices operate within the grounded theory model of urban mission practice, 
revealing tensions between openness and efficiency, and between experimentation and 
institutional conformity.
Regen Melbourne: Earthshots
Regen Melbourne emerged in 2019 after the Black Summer bushfires and COVID-19 lockdowns, 
positioning itself as a collaborative platform to reimagine the city’s future. Drawing on Doughnut 
Economics, it set out three “Earthshots” – Swimmable Birrarung (Yarra River), 300,000 Streets, 
and Nourished Neighbourhoods – as bold declarations of intent. These Earthshots illustrate the 
practice of constructing shared vocabularies that mobilise ambition and connect disparate actors.
Capturing the power of framing practices, the interviewee described Earthshots as a deliberate 
departure from bounded “project” language, adopting a lexicon that enabled communities to 
see how their “tiny little things” contribute to systemic transformation. The framing of 300,000 
Streets is, for instance, recast streets as an interconnected web, allowing language to travel 
across altitudes by grounding abstract strategy in lived realities for policymakers, while inspiring 
community leaders through accessible metaphors. In this way, framing and stewardship  
converged, relying on trust to carry messages between scales.
These narratives also functioned as sensemaking tools, co-produced through engagement 
with more than 500 stakeholders and reinforced in demonstrator sites where change could be 
“walked around, felt and seen.” Yet ambition itself introduced tension. The rhetorical power of 
Earthshots made them vulnerable to narrative capture, where institutions adopted the language 
without substantive follow-through, while their scale required constant anchoring in tangible 
action to avoid drifting into abstraction. Regen Melbourne illustrates how framing practices 
operate within the grounded theory model of urban mission practice, showing both their 
generative capacity and the tensions between inspiration, narrative capture, and tangible action.
Action Lab: The Future of Home Lab
The Future of Home Lab, led by Skills Society's Action Lab with Inclusion Alberta, Civida, and 
Homeward Trust, explored how housing and supports could be reimagined to enable inclusion for 
people with developmental disabilities. Rather than a fixed project, the Lab worked through an 
"Innovation Swirl" – cycling research, prototyping, and pilots to make "small bets before big bets" 
and stay responsive to lived realities within the “messy middle”. This expressed stewardship  as 
strategic agility, while tools such as the Three Horizons framework linked immediate experiments 
to longer-term system change and helped maintain "scope or die" discipline about what the Lab 
could influence.
As the work unfolded, teams shifted altitudes, translating between families' experiences, service 
practice, and policy and finance settings. Sensemaking  and framing were intertwined: prototypes 
and scenarios enabled collective exploration; shared narratives helped diverse actors orient 
around possible futures. The Lab's governance – a Stewardship Team, Project Partners, and 
Core Teams – provided enough structure to hold the relationships and the tensions between lived 
experience and evidence-based perspectives.
Trust was treated as infrastructure. Participants stressed that "All of the systems change stuff, 
it's about relationships," and that building them takes time, even when that slows near-term 
progress. The need to go slow to go far sat in tension with pressures for delivery and clarity of 
outcomes. Equally, balancing a clear direction with openness to emergent results was a continual 
test of stewardship .
The Future of Home Lab illustrates how stewardship , sensemaking , and framing practices 
operate within the grounded theory model of urban mission practice, surfacing tensions between 
direction and emergence, and between slow trust-building and demands for delivery.

practices that adapt and translate evidence so climate ambitions move from aspiration to

5. Discussion
5.1 What are the practices of systemic, niche, and process 
intermediaries in the design, adaptation, and negotiation of urban 
missions at the neighbourhood scale?
This study identifies four dimensions of urban mission practice – dynamic stewardship, shaping, 
sensemaking, and framing – articulating nine routines through which systemic, niche, and 
process intermediaries design, adapt, and negotiate missions. These practices interact in 
continuous feedback loops, configuring the socio-spatial dimensions of neighbourhoods – social 
infrastructure, streets, nature, and governance (Pagh & Cook, 2023). Rather than abstract 
functions, they represent everyday routines that both generate new capacities and expose 
tensions. 
Dynamic stewardship  sits at the centre of these dimensions, orchestrating governance and 
social infrastructures to enable neighbourhoods as sites of systemic transformation. Practitioners 
embed trust across system altitudes, addressing seizing deficits by convening coalitions and 
translating lived experience into policy-relevant signals. These routines align fragmented 
initiatives into coherent portfolios while wrestling with efficiency logics that undervalue the slow, 
relational work of trust – creating democratic legitimacy and governance infrastructures capable 
of holding missions together. 
If stewardship provides orchestration, shaping  gives missions physical and social form, 
creating infrastructures where transformation can be contested, adapted, or sustained. Through 
participatory scaffolding and street-level prototyping, intermediaries embed experimentation in 
everyday life. Yet participation is never frictionless: open infrastructures invite conflict, impose 
uneven burdens, and rarely self-reinforce, making shaping  as much about sustaining social 
infrastructures as embedding physical change.
Sensemaking  grounds missions in reflexivity, embedding evidence into neighbourhood 
governance and, in climate-focused initiatives, into the nature dimension. Distinctive here is not 
the collection of data but its curation and translation. By blending “heavy” quantitative measures 
with “thick” qualitative insights – as in Leuven 2030’s iterative CO₂ inventories – intermediaries 
connect systemic targets with lived realities. This mitigates weak sensing capabilities and 
embeds adaptive learning, preventing paralysis by static metrics and ensuring missions remain 
responsive to ecological and social realities.
Finally, framing mobilises collective purpose, cutting across all socio-spatial dimensions to 
shape how transformation is imagined, legitimised, and connected across scales. Intermediaries 
construct vocabularies and narratives that resonate across audiences, giving missions legitimacy 
and direction. However, these routines carry risks: exclusion or co-option can reinforce dominant 
interests rather than broaden participation, placing significant responsibility on practitioners to 
ensure framing expands rather than narrows collective imagination.
Together, these four dimensions show how intermediary practices reconfigure the socio-spatial 
fabric of neighbourhoods – as defined by Pagh & Cook (2023). They demonstrate that urban 
missions take shape not through abstract roles or functions but through interdependent routines 
of orchestration, prototyping, reflexivity, and narrative-building – translating systemic ambitions 
into lived neighbourhood settings while contending with frictions, burdens, and uncertainties of 
everyday life. What makes these practices significant is their distinct socio-spatial lens, working 
within, between and across each dimension.
5.2 An emerging typology of urban mission practice
While the grounded theory model identifies four dimensions and nine practices through which 
intermediaries engage with the socio-spatial dimensions of mission neighbourhoods, it remains 
an empirical account of practice. To systematise these findings and enable comparison, the 
model has been developed into an emerging typology of urban mission practice (Table 7).
Following Dorst’s (201 1) guidance on design-oriented typologies, the “types” are the four 
practice dimensions identified in the grounded model – dynamic stewardship, shaping, 
sensemaking, and framing. Each type is distinguished by the interplay of practices (the routines 
identified), contexts (the socio-spatial dimensions they reconfigure), problems  (capability deficits 
they address), and actors  (systemic, niche, and process intermediaries), with outcomes showing 
how they contribute to the formation of mission neighbourhoods.

This emerging typology makes two theoretical advances. First, it provides a practice-based 
articulation of how urban missions address weak sensing, seizing, and transforming capabilities 
that characterise the “missing middle” of urban governance (Kattel, 2022; Mazzucato, 2024). In 
doing so, it reframes abstract intermediary functions as situated, routinised practices, highlighting 
their generative capacities: building civic capacity (seizing), embedding experimentation 
(transforming), cultivating reflexivity (sensing), and mobilising collective purpose (directionality/
legitimacy). 
Second, it illustrates how these practices overlap, complement, and at times contest one another 
across socio-spatial infrastructures. Governance emerges through the interplay of stewardship  
and sensemaking ; stewardship and shaping foster civic capacity through participatory 
infrastructures; and sensemaking  and framing blend ecological evidence with regenerative 
narratives. These overlaps demonstrate that mission neighbourhoods materialise not through 
discrete practices but through patterned interdependencies – as civic, material, reflexive, and 
narrative spaces.
In this way, the emerging typology moves beyond description to offer an analytical framework 
that clarifies the generative capacities of intermediary practices, surfaces their tensions (slow 
trust-building, uneven participation, reductive metrics, narrative capture), and extends existing 
accounts of intermediary functions and design capabilities into a systematic practice-based 
model. This positions the emerging typology as a bridge between empirical studies of situated 
practice and abstract accounts of intermediary roles, offering a framework that can be tested, 
adapted, and one day institutionalised in future research and policy.
5.3 From capabilities and functions: towards a synthesis of urban 
mission practices
While existing literature on public sector innovation and transition intermediaries has highlighted 
orders, capabilities, and functions, it offers limited empirical insight into how these unfold in 
practice at the neighbourhood scale. This emerging typology can help fill this gap by providing a 
practice-based framework that can be situated against, and extend, existing conceptualisations – 
moving closer to a synthesis of urban mission practice.
First, the emerging typology addresses a gap in existing accounts of intermediary practice by 
extending Von Wirth et al. (2019) diffusion-focused account. Rather than emphasising how 
practices spread, the typology specifies the mechanisms that underpin diffusion itself – coalition-
building, prototyping, curating evidence, and constructing narratives – suggesting how these are 
embedded in socio-spatial contexts. 
Next, the typology can be interpreted through Sørensen & Torfing’s (2022) three orders of 
public innovation as a socio-spatial articulation of how these orders unfold at the neighbourhood 
scale. Shaping aligns with first-order change by materialising ambitions through prototypes, 
civic infrastructures, and physical spaces; stewardship  and framing align with second-order 
innovation by reconfiguring collaborative processes through coalitions and shared vocabularies; 
and sensemaking, when stewardship becomes institutionalised, enables third-order innovation by 
embedding reflexive cycles of testing and learning into governance arrangements.
This mapping can then be further enriched through Hill & Melander’s (2025) concept of pace 
layers, which shows how practices operate across different scales and tempos of change. For 
example, if Sørensen & Torfing describe vertical orders of innovation from services to institutions, 
pace layers turn the model horizontally, revealing their systemic and temporal breadth (Figure 6).

5.4 Mission neighbourhoods: epistemic or normative?
Both the grounded theory model and the emerging typology raise questions about whether 
mission neighbourhoods function as epistemic or normative initiatives, and, to an extent, in which 
domain they should remain. Normatively, missions provide directionality – setting goals, mobilising 
actors, and legitimising interventions in the name of societal challenges. Epistemically, however, 
they demand a shift in how knowledge is created and used: embedding reflexivity, disrupting 
incumbent logics, and experimenting with new ways of knowing (Kattel & Mazzucato, 2025). 
While the findings show glimpses of such an epistemic turn, particularly in shaping practices that 
build collaborative, participatory infrastructures, these remain fragile and contested.
As discussed in the theoretical background (Section 2.2), while neighbourhoods are increasingly 
positioned as the “ideal scale” for mobilising systemic transformation, policy and urban planning 
has typically defaulted to neoliberal and technocratic approaches – using incumbent analytical 
tools such as cost–benefit evaluation and efficiency-driven agendas (Pagh & Cook, 2023; 
Mazzucato & Ryan-Collins, 2022; Kattel & Mazzucato, 2025). These approaches privilege 
growth and efficiency over complexity and local meaning, leaving urban missions vulnerable 
to reductionism. Similarly, Section 2.3 highlights the weak dynamic capabilities of public 
organisations at the urban scale: poor sensing (spatial blindness), poor seizing (coordination 
deficits), and poor transforming (absence of generative institutions). Without targeting these 
capability gaps, urban missions are likely to reproduce business-as-usual routines rather than 
shifting epistemic foundations – regardless of the role of intermediaries (Kattel & Mazzucato, 
2025). 
In practice, for example, while intermediaries may steward coalitions, shape participatory 
infrastructures, curate evidence, and construct narratives, these practices can slide into symbolic 
participation rather than systemic change. Efforts to mobilise against racial inequality have 
persisted for more than a decade with little structural improvement; Leuven 2030 demonstrates 
measurable local climate progress, yet in the context of worsening global emissions, such 
achievements risk being eclipsed. As Kattel & Mazzucato (2025) argue, unless capability and 
analytical gaps are addressed, experimentation within missions will remain hampered and 
vulnerable to being overrun by political events or conventional technocratic logic.
5.5 Limitations and future research
This study provides a grounded but necessarily bounded snapshot of four case studies,examining 
how systemic, niche, and process intermediaries design, adapt, and negotiate missions at the 
neighbourhood scale. While a grounded theory approach enables the inductive development of 
a practice-based typology, it also constrains the findings to the specific contexts and timeframe 
studied (Charmaz, 2006). The framework and typology should therefore be treated as emerging 
models to be tested, refined, and extended.

6. Conclusion
This study has explored the practices of systemic, niche, and process intermediaries in the 
design, adaptation, and negotiation of urban missions at the neighbourhood scale. Using a 
grounded theory approach, it identified four dimensions of urban mission practice – dynamic 
stewardship, shaping, sensemaking , and framing – articulating nine interrelated routines. Across 
case studies, these practices were shown to reconfigure the socio-spatial dimensions of 
neighbourhoods, translating systemic ambitions into civic capacity, participatory infrastructures, 
reflexive governance, and shared narratives of change.
The findings extend existing literature by moving beyond abstract functions of intermediation to 
detail the everyday routines through which mission neighbourhoods are formed. The emerging 
typology highlights how practices overlap, complement, and at times contest each other, while 
also exposing their limits: trust takes time and care to embed, participation can impose uneven 
demands, evidence risks being reduced to static metrics, and narratives may be shaped or 
captured by dominant actors. In doing so, the study provides a framework for linking practice-
based insights to broader accounts of innovation orders, dynamic capabilities, and design 
capabilities. 
Ultimately, while intermediaries make systemic ambitions tangible at the neighbourhood scale, 
their practices remain precarious without institutionalisation. For policymakers and practitioners, 
this underlines the importance of resourcing and embedding intermediary work into governance 
processes so that stewardship , shaping, sensemaking , and framing practices do not remain 
isolated efforts in the “missing middle”. Unless urban missions move beyond direction-setting 
towards institutionalising these practices, their transformative potential will remain limited. 
Supporting intermediaries to stabilise their routines across systems and scales is therefore 
crucial to turning mission neighbourhoods into robust, lasting infrastructures for systemic change.
Given the dynamic nature of practices, missions, and neighbourhoods, future research would 
benefit from longitudinal studies tracing how practices evolve over time and under shifting 
conditions – alongside the development of urban mission initiatives. As discussed in Section 
2.3, public organisations face persistent capability deficits in sensing, seizing, and transforming. 
It remains unclear whether the practices identified here stabilise into durable capabilities, 
dissolve under pressure, or adapt in unexpected ways. Such inquiry could also highlight the 
failure of these practices, showing whether trust consolidates or erodes, or whether participatory 
infrastructures scale, dissipate, or generate fatigue.
Comparative studies across geographies and governance systems would also be valuable. As 
highlighted in Section 2.5, existing practice-based accounts remain highly contextual, and this 
study similarly reflects its specific cases with its small sample size. Cross-national or cross-
sectoral comparison could test whether stewardship, shaping, sensemaking, and framing emerge 
elsewhere, and how socio-spatial configurations shape their form and effectiveness.
Finally, future research should examine the interdependencies between practices. The findings 
suggest that stewardship underpins shaping, and sensemaking supports framing, but sequencing 
and mutual reinforcement remain poorly understood. Addressing these questions would advance 
understanding of whether mission practices can move beyond bounded adaptations to become 
institutionalised capabilities for systemic transformation.
