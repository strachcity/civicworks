# Mission-oriented city–industry transformations

*Why setting a mission turns strategy into commitment, and what cities must build to hold it*

Jack Strachan, Senior Designer, CustomerFirst, UK Government Digital Service

## Abstract

City-level missions give industrial strategy somewhere to land, turning broad ambitions for systemic change into commitments people and institutions can recognise and act on. This paper examines how cities diagnose, frame, build coalitions, experiment and steward missions in practice, and how that work builds institutional capacity. That capacity is often relational and tacit. It sits between people, becomes difficult to retain when programmes end, travels badly between places, and costs most to rebuild in cities with the least room to spare. Innovation Scaling Corridors respond to this problem by providing shared infrastructure for carrying learning and capability between cities, while keeping missions locally owned.

## 1. What, and who, is a city for?

Over the past century, cities have sat at the centre of industrial transformation, economic growth and job creation, shaped by those who live in, govern, finance, build and profit from them. While city models have changed, from 20th-century industrial and modernist planning to 21st-century smart cities and innovation districts, urban development has often been made governable by separating the city into different functions of production, settlement, infrastructure and growth.[^1] Yet cities have always been places of complexity, rich with memory and conflict, where these functions have never been truly separable. And as the pace and scale of change grow, that separation becomes harder to sustain because the decisions cities face increasingly cut across the boundaries through which they have been organised.

Take a city deciding to decarbonise its port. It is an easy intent to announce, but much harder to organise in practice. The transition depends on shipping firms, the utility that owns the substations, the regulator setting emissions rules, the colleges retraining the workforce, and the dock workers whose livelihoods sit inside the old way of doing things. Sharpening incentives would help, as would removing barriers that make cleaner technology expensive to adopt, but neither answers the harder question of how jobs, investment, infrastructure, regulation, skills and enough public trust can be organised so that the transition holds. This is the problem many city leaders now find themselves trying to solve.

Mission-oriented innovation offers one way of working with this complexity. It starts from the idea that growth has a direction as well as a rate, and that public investment, procurement and regulation can shape markets and capabilities towards decarbonisation, resilience or inclusion rather than only correcting markets when they fail.[^2][^3] That direction also raises the question of who growth is for. In a city, that question is hard to keep abstract because a city cannot exist only to serve businesses, investment or infrastructure. It also has to sustain the lives, livelihoods and services that make growth socially meaningful, and through which industrial transformation can contribute to progress towards the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).[^4] Cities, of course, already act as economic agents through planning, land use, procurement, regulation and public investment. Setting a mission gives that agency a direction to organise around, holding jobs, infrastructure, investment and trust together long enough for a shared purpose to survive contact with many challenges at once.

For city leaders, then, the starting point is understanding where and how a problem runs through the city, turning that understanding into a shared direction, and organising the actors, investments and experiments needed to move towards it. Choosing that direction is also a choice about what, and who, the city is being organised for. A city-scale mission turns that direction into a commitment a city can act on. The harder work begins once a city tries to put the commitment into practice.

## 2. What does a mission look like at a city scale?

While national and supranational mission-oriented policy can provide shared direction, time horizons and measurable outcomes, it is also, necessarily, abstract. It needs to be broad enough to legitimise action across sectors and places within a country, while carrying a vision strong enough to mobilise citizens, businesses and public institutions. That breadth is, of course, part of its democratic and political function. But it also comes at a cost.

National missions are not built to hold the specific conditions that shape whether transformation is possible in any one place, meaning they can quickly become spatially blind to local realities.[^A][^5][^6] A national mission to decarbonise heavy industry may be politically powerful, but it reads the same on paper whether it lands in a port city of 200,000 or a steel town of 40,000, and cannot account for the place-specific assets, relationships and dependencies that determine what is possible in either place. City-level missions provide an essential mechanism for translation, tying national-scale missions’ broad public direction to a specific geography, and to the industries, institutions and communities through which transformation has to happen. The basic logic stays the same. What changes is the specificity a mission has to hold, and the connective work that specificity demands.

This is where governance becomes a defining feature of city-level industrial missions, because the systems being transformed rarely sit within the authority of city government alone. The task for cities is this connective work, aligning the businesses, utilities, regulators and workers whose separate decisions have to move together for transformation to happen. At a national scale, those actors arrive as categories, sectors and associations. At a city scale they have names, assets and relationships that can be organised around a shared purpose. Ownership and expertise remain scattered, but the trade-offs are harder to hide because they land on people and places that can be identified. A city-level mission becomes real by organising those relationships so that what is agreed in the room still holds outside it.

Skip that work and a mission stays a strategy, a slogan or a political announcement dressed in more ambitious language. What converts a strategy into a commitment is that the mission stops living in a document and starts being held by the people who have to act on them. A strategy can state a city’s ambition and how it intends to get there. A mission has to name who is doing what, how progress will be measured, where investment and delivery capacity will come from and how learning will change the work over time, all in terms the people involved have agreed to be held to. This is the gritty, on-the-ground work of arranging the conditions that let a strategy become real in a place.

The EU Cities Mission is the clearest large-scale test of what governing place-based missions involves.[^7][^8] It is a climate mission that is also an industrial one, rewiring energy systems, retraining workforces and redirecting infrastructure investment across more than a hundred cities working towards climate neutrality by 2030. Each participating city negotiates a Climate City Contract (CCC), turning the 2030 target into commitments and investment plans that its own institutions have signed up to (Box 1). While the framework is common, what it demands differs once it meets a real city, its institutions, its industrial history and its conflicts.

### Box 1. What a Climate City Contract asks of a city[^9]

Each city that participates in the EU Cities Mission develops a Climate City Contract. What the contract does is make a city write down, in public and alongside the institutions it depends on, what climate neutrality would require of each of them. The contracts are designed as an ongoing process rather than a document filed once, and NetZeroCities describes it as a governance innovation tool. It has three linked parts:

- **The Commitments** set out the city’s 2030 goal, three or four priorities targeting systemic change in high-emission sectors, and a formal contract signed by the stakeholders involved, with a second section recording what each of them has individually undertaken to do.
- **The Action Plan** works from an agreed baseline and a gap analysis to a coordinated portfolio of interventions, with timelines, responsibilities and indicators attached.
- **The Investment Plan** sets out the capital needed and how it will be mobilised, across municipal budgets, EU and national funding, and private capital.

Contracts are assessed by the European Commission, which may award the EU Mission Label. Of 112 Mission Cities, 103 held the Label in August 2026.

Leuven and Velenje show what that difference looks like in practice: both are working through the same European mission framework, but they enter it with very different histories, relationships and institutional capacity. Leuven, in Belgium, did not have to build a coalition to negotiate its CCC.[^10] Leuven 2030, a multi-stakeholder initiative supporting the city’s green transition, had already spent a decade building relationships across city government, business and civil society, so when its Climate City Contract arrived much of the relational work was already in place. Velenje, in Slovenia, is building that capacity under very different conditions.[^11][^12] Its economy is built around coal mining, and in January 2022 the national government adopted a phase-out strategy under which the mine will close by 2033 at the latest. This made economic transformation an urgent issue for the city. Climate neutrality has to be negotiated with the workers and communities whose livelihoods remain tied to the industrial system the city is trying to transform. Even with a plan in place, city officials describe winning over the first generation of miners as one of the hardest parts of the work.

Where Leuven had a decade to build its coalition before the contract ever asked anything of it, Velenje is building its coalition while asking a mining town to imagine a future beyond the industry that shaped it. That difference matters. Who gets pulled in, when, and on what terms is not a footnote to the mission. It is the mission, translated into what one city’s history allows and another city’s does not.

### Box 2. Leuven and Velenje: mission building in practice[^10][^11]

#### Leuven

Leuven 2030 began with a report rather than a structure. In early 2013, the city and KU Leuven set out a scientific path to a climate-neutral Leuven, and among the report’s recommendations was to create a multi-stakeholder initiative to enable the transition. By that autumn the platform existed, co-founded by the city and more than fifty other stakeholders.

Its governance is deliberately flat. Six stakeholder groups sit in its decision-making bodies: citizens, civil society, companies, knowledge institutions, the city and its agencies, and public and semi-public institutions. Each holds formal representation and the same number of votes, so strategy is set on an equal footing. The platform is funded mainly by its members and run by a team of around eight.

In 2019 the platform completed a Roadmap, 13 programmes and 80 action clusters, co-created with 60 local experts and practitioners and formally signed by 15 key stakeholders. To implement it, Leuven 2030 recruited 17 programme facilitators from across member organisations, each mobilising action within one programme and defining the breakthrough projects intended to clear structural barriers. That team was disbanded at the start of 2023 due to lack of resources.

Leuven’s own account of the period notes that stakeholder engagement remains central to the platform, and that future efforts will focus on using public commitments to create greater transparency and accountability.

#### Velenje

Velenje is a city of around 33,700 people in the Šaleška valley of Slovenia, planned and built in the 1950s around lignite. The mine, the last operating in Slovenia, supplies the Šoštanj thermal power plant in the neighbouring municipality. Along with the appliance manufacturer Hisense Gorenje, the mine is one of the city’s two largest employers, the pair accounting for over 4,000 jobs.

Velenje set the goal of an 80% cut in emissions against its 2018 baseline by 2030. Its central challenge is the district heating system, built in 1959 and serving around 40,000 users and 650 industrial clients across three municipalities. Its heat comes from Šoštanj, so the phase-out that closes the mine also removes the city’s heat supply, and coal extraction is being sustained in part to keep those users warm until a replacement exists. Replacing the heat and replacing the jobs are the same piece of work.

To run the contract process, the city appointed a Transition Team drawn from key municipal departments, the public utility company and the local energy agency. The action plan is treated as a live document rather than a fixed one, monitored by that team, with a Strategic Council to oversee implementation. The just transition work runs alongside it through the Just Transition Centre at the SAŠA regional development agency.

## 3. How do city-level missions work in practice?

As Leuven and Velenje illustrate, city missions do not begin when a mayor announces a target. Defining a mission and practising one are two different problems, and knowing what a mission is does not tell you how to build one. Diagnosis shapes framing, framing shapes who joins the coalition, coalition shapes what gets tested and testing reveals what actually has to change. Connective work runs through each step, though coalition-building is where much of that work is deliberately organised. This is the real, hairy, knotty work of city-based mission practice –learning under uncertainty while trying to produce transformation.

Missions start with understanding a city’s industrial system. Cities often reach for asset maps, sector surveys and investment inventories that make a system look more governable than it is, producing clean maps of messy realities. A useful diagnosis asks harder questions: who benefits and bears the costs, where does formal and informal power sit, which capabilities exist locally, how do national policy, finance and regulation shape what is possible, and underneath all of it, where might genuine leverage be found.[^B] That kind of diagnosis is not a desktop exercise. It means testing early with the people who hold different parts of the system: businesses on costs, workers and unions on skills and job security, utilities on capacity, agencies on regulation and finance, community organisations on trust. It is a practice of sensemaking that continues through the life of the mission.

How a mission is framed matters as much as what it commits to, because framing shapes how people see themselves in a mission, who needs to act, and what kinds of action become possible. A mission built around climate neutrality may make sense to policymakers, but one built around a river, a neighbourhood or a port can be easier for people to recognise, act on and organise around, because it is what they know, see and experience in their daily lives.[^C] The same discipline applies to businesses and investors for whom a mission becomes actionable only when it is legible as future demand, a pipeline of work and propositions worth investing in rather than an ambition worth applauding. A frame that works has to hold both registers at once.

Coalition-building is never finished, and it cannot begin after the mission has already been written. It is the continuous practice of building relational infrastructure, negotiated before a mission exists, tested while it is being built, and renewed long after it is launched. Cities that announce a mission and then invite stakeholders to align behind it often discover that the actors who were not part of shaping the work do not feel bound by it. They nod along in the room, then go back to their desks and carry on as before. This is how mission-washing starts, quietly, in the relationships that were never built beneath the language or the ambition.[^13] It is why businesses, utilities, workers, universities and public agencies need more than a seat at the table. They need the people in between, who love their city enough to convene the room, broker relationships and hold trust over time.

These people also help make a coalition more inclusive, reaching the small businesses and residents neither a university nor city hall can reach on its own. Citizen Hub, a “social infrastructure company” working out of a former job centre in St Neots, England, is a small example of this, sustaining relationships and building capability within the community beyond individual programmes and funding cycles.[^D] In a city-industrial mission, that kind of connective capacity can keep businesses, institutions and residents in the work long enough to find terms they can commit to.

Missions advance through projects, procurements and services, through which an ambition becomes something a city can test at a small scale in real conditions. A city trying to reach net-zero, for example, may decide that electrifying its municipal bus fleet is one place to begin, but the first move might be just a handful of buses on one route, tested against real weather, maintenance schedules and everyday use. Starting small lets a city learn more than whether a technology works. It reveals which actors are serious partners, which regulations quietly block progress, whether people trust the change enough to keep using it, and what conflicts surface once real jobs and routines are involved. This is the dark matter of a city’s industrial system, the parts no asset map ever shows, visible only once something real has been tried.[^E][^14][^15] What gets learned through one small intervention is what a mission needs before it can responsibly ask a wider system to change. How that learning is converted into something a city can commission, finance and deliver at scale is a harder question, and part of the answer may not sit inside a single city at all.

Stewardship is what a mission needs to hold a direction steady while the conditions around it keep moving. Running a city through the work of meeting a mission is closer to sailing a boat while still building it. This is the difference between technocratic performance management and active stewardship, between asking whether a plan is on track and asking what the work is teaching and whether the original direction still holds. The Just Transition Centre working with Velenje learned this directly. Its first campaign for free district-heating upgrades stalled at 45% of buildings. Rather than repeat the same campaign louder, the Centre worked out who still needed convincing and what was stopping them before returning building by building. The result: signed contracts passed 80%.[^16] That is stewardship in practice, noticing when an approach has failed, learning why, and having the standing and mandate to try something else.[^F][^17]

## 4. Building the capacity to sustain missions

The deeper a city gets into a mission, the more it has to reconfigure around what the work reveals. Because of this, institutional capacity has to develop alongside the mission itself.[^G][^18][^19] Much of that capacity gets built through doing the work. Someone discovers which rules that look binding on paper actually bite, how a decision really moves when formal responsibility sits somewhere else and which relationships have to be built across boundaries no organogram would predict. Over time, a team develops a feel for how the institution actually works, and for how far it can push before something breaks. Often that judgement accumulates inside the temporary room a programme or funding settlement opens up, where people have permission to work differently while they find out what the mission is going to demand of the institution.

The harder problem is what remains when that room closes. Most of what has been learned is tacit, held between people rather than written down, and bound up in connections that only work because of who built them. People return to roles organised around different priorities, relationships become harder to activate, and practical knowledge can survive more readily in a report than in the way the institution operates. A city can finish a piece of work knowing far more than it did at the start, yet the next team still starts by asking questions the previous team answered two years ago.

The same problem appears between cities. What another city needs is rarely an answer stripped of its history. It needs enough context to understand why something worked and what would have to change before it could be useful somewhere else.[^H][^20] Somebody has to carry that context between places, and too often nobody is responsible for doing it.

The cost of rebuilding that capacity falls very differently between cities. Larger city governments may have enough people and institutional room to carry relationships and knowledge between programmes. Smaller municipalities have much less capacity to spare, and in many developing and emerging economies the ambition to shape industrial change sits alongside limited specialist capacity and very little discretionary resource to build it with. The cities that would gain most from new capacity can face the highest relative cost of making room for it.

Even a well-resourced city will eventually reach the edge of what it can hold alone. A pilot can work well on one street or one bus route and then meet an entirely different set of constraints when the city tries to scale it. Those constraints often extend beyond the city, in suppliers for whom one city’s order is too small to be worth changing production for,[^I] regulations it can influence without directing, and skills or infrastructure systems that stretch beyond its boundary. That raises a bigger question for city leaders. What does a city need to hold for itself, and what becomes more useful when cities hold it together?

## 5. What cities can hold together: Innovation Scaling Corridors

Innovation Scaling Corridors offer a way of holding some of that capacity between cities, while each city keeps its own mission, political choices and accountability.[^J][^21][^22][^23] A city should not have to rebuild every procurement capability, financing route, institutional relationship or lesson from scratch when another place has already worked through something similar. Carrying that learning between places is itself work, akin to what the people in between do inside a city, holding context, recognising related constraints and helping another place read what was learned. Done well, it can also leave more behind for the next team in the city where the learning began.

The work of the corridor is to find where those connections are useful while leaving each mission rooted in the place that gave it meaning. Corridors make it possible to see where different cities depend on the same suppliers, need capabilities another place already holds, or create enough combined demand to shift a market.

How far that can go is still unsettled. Some forms of knowledge and capability travel more easily than others. Relationships, judgement, political standing and the institutional memory built through working in a particular place travel much less cleanly. That is part of why each city needs to keep hold of its own mission rather than inherit somebody else’s. Which capabilities become stronger when cities hold them together, and how much of a place has to travel with what it has learned before somewhere else can use it well, are questions a corridor can only answer in practice.

## 6. From local missions to shared capacity

For a city leader, the starting point is a problem that matters locally and whose effects run through the local economy. From there, the work is to understand where the city has influence, bring in the institutions and industries shaping the problem, and begin testing where change is possible. What lets a mission survive is the capacity built through that work. The gain comes when that capacity accumulates, so the next piece of work starts from somewhere further on.

ScaleX is beginning to test whether some of that capacity can be built and held differently through the first Innovation Scaling Corridor in the Western Balkans. For cities with little institutional room to spare, or without an established regional structure to connect into, a corridor can provide capability and connections that would be difficult to assemble alone. Working alongside local teams before transferring operations and ownership is how the question gets answered in practice, since it is only in the doing that it becomes clear which capacities are usefully held between cities and which have to stay close to the place itself.

A mission gives a city a direction, and the capacity built around this mission is what lets the city keep hold of that direction while everything the work reveals keeps changing underneath. A city that can do that becomes something more than a place where national industrial strategy is delivered. It becomes one of the agents through which that strategy finds traction in the economy it is trying to change.
